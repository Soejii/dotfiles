# Material Modes

Select the mode that matches the user's target. Combine modes only when the material genuinely crosses them.

## Assignment or outline

Explain the actual deliverable, the central question, why each section exists, and how the sections form one argument. Identify scope mismatches, hidden assumptions, missing transitions, unclear ownership in group work, and claims that require evidence. Distinguish lecturer requirements from a group's chosen framing.

## Textbook chapter

Identify the problem the chapter addresses, its section map, the progression of ideas, and the few concepts that carry most of the chapter. Separate essential arguments from examples, historical detail, and enrichment. Explain prerequisites before concepts that depend on them.

## Academic paper

Explain the research question, motivation, theoretical basis, design, participants or data, measures, findings, limitations, and practical meaning. Distinguish what the study found from what the authors speculate. Do not treat an abstract, literature review citation, or secondary summary as proof of a stronger claim than it supports.

## Theory or concept

Explain the problem the concept solves, its definition, mechanism, components, contrasts, applications, and boundaries. When two concepts sound similar, compare the exact dimension on which they differ rather than offering two isolated definitions.

## Transcript or voice-note transcript

Separate speaker instructions, group decisions, interpretations, factual claims, uncertainty, and action items. A statement in a voice note proves what the speaker requested or believed, not that the academic claim itself is true. Flag claims that need a citable source.

## Multiple sources

Synthesize where the sources agree, differ, or operate at different levels of analysis. Do not flatten distinct definitions into a false consensus. State which source supports each important claim and where the synthesis is your inference.
